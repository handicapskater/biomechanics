What does this mean for lifelong mobility?

Mobility should evolve with us.

Mobility is not just about whether a person can walk. It is about preserving useful function while managing the mechanical demands placed on the body over time.

HandicapSkater compares walking with controlled rolling mobility to ask whether different movement strategies can preserve function with different mechanical patterns.

Movement is not the same as force

Kinematics describes how the body moves: trajectories, segment motion, velocity, acceleration and joint articulation.

Kinetics describes forces and mechanical loads that produce or result from movement, including ground-reaction forces and joint moments.

In the repeated same-day comparisons, Walking is associated with higher vertical RMS and jerk than Mall skating. These measurements do not establish internal joint-force reduction.

What the sensors measure · How controlled rolling differs

Controlled rolling changes the movement strategy

Skating permits controlled rolling rather than repeated walking steps. Ankle, knee and hip articulation may redistribute or attenuate mechanical inputs differently. That is a biomechanical hypothesis, not a direct measurement of internal joint loading or eliminated SI-joint shock.

Independent biomechanics context: Mahar et al. (1997)

Mahar et al. compared treadmill inline skating with running and reported lower tibial and head impact acceleration during skating. Running is not identical to this case’s Walking protocol. This reference supplies independent context, not a measurement of Troy’s internal joint forces. See the existing biomechanics explanation.

Keep measurement and interpretation separate

What was measured—and what was not

Functional distance and duration describe useful activity. Vertical RMS, jerk, acceleration-derived transients and event/tail morphology describe on-body mechanical behavior. The Mall skating → Walking → PT skating protocol compares stages on the same day.

Current telemetry measures chest/on-body acceleration-derived behavior and mechanical proxies. It does not directly measure ground-reaction force, SI-joint force, hip joint contact force, internal pelvic force, tissue stress, or pain.

Chest transient-event frequency is not pelvic or SI-joint load. Potential lower-extremity attenuation is a hypothesis; the current chest sensor cannot localize it.

A longer human lifespan makes functional lifespan more important

How can people preserve useful mobility when walking becomes painful, inefficient or mechanically costly?

Mobility evolution means evaluating strategies by function, mechanical behavior, physiological response, safety, individual capability and context—not limiting the discussion to canes, walkers and wheelchairs.

This is a forward-looking question. No longevity benefit of skating or of lower RMS/jerk has been established by this evidence.

Historical evidence and a separate prospective test

Historical Core and PVC-01 remain separate evidence layers; their sample sizes are not pooled. PVC-01 was frozen before five post-lock dates—September 6–10, 2026—were examined.

  • Mall→Walking distance: frozen direction reproduced on 5/5 dates.
  • Walking→PT distance: 5/5.
  • Mall→Walking vertical RMS: 5/5.
  • Mall→Walking jerk: 5/5.
  • Mall→Walking chest shock-spike rate: 5/5.
  • Walking→PT chest shock-spike rate: lower-PT direction reproduced on 1/5 dates.

No family-level success threshold was frozen. The nonreplicated chest shock endpoint remains visible; it does not negate PT function or establish pelvic loading.
